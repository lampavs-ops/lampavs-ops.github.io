## A Show Nobody Was Watching

Let me tell you about one of the great word-of-mouth success stories in recent K-drama history.

On June 29, 2022, a new legal drama premiered in Korea. It aired on a channel called ENA. Almost nobody had heard of ENA. It was a small cable channel, not one of the big broadcasters.

The first episode drew a nationwide rating of 0.9%. That is not a hit. That is a rounding error.

Then people started telling their friends. By the third episode the show hit 4.0%, which was already the highest rating in ENA's entire history. The numbers climbed every single week after that.

The finale, on August 18, scored an average nationwide rating of 17.5% (Nielsen Korea, paid household basis). In the Seoul metropolitan area it reached 19.2%.

That is a jump of more than sixteen points from first episode to last. It remains the highest rating any program has ever earned on ENA, a channel that had been on air since 2004 without a hit anywhere near this size.

Its growth was driven largely by word of mouth and fast-spreading online attention. *Deadline*, tracking the Netflix numbers week by week that summer, described the show's rise the same way. Nobody could have scheduled that.

This is Part 1 of our three-part series. This post is **completely spoiler-free**, so you can read it before you watch a single episode.

## Who Is Woo Young-woo?

Woo Young-woo is a rookie lawyer. She is 27. She graduated at the top of her class at Seoul National University, in college and then in law school. Her memory is close to photographic. She can recite a full legal code from a book she read years ago.

She is also autistic.

Because of that, law firm after law firm turned her down. She is eventually hired by Hanbada, a huge Seoul law firm, after its CEO pushes to bring her in over the doubts of a skeptical senior attorney. So the show begins on her first day at work, where she is both the smartest person in the room and the person everyone is least sure about.

Here is what makes her so easy to love:

**The palindromes.** Her name reads the same forward and backward. When she introduces herself, she says so — and then lists other palindromes as proof. In the English subtitles the list is: kayak, deed, rotator, noon, racecar, Woo Young-woo. In the original Korean she says words that are palindromes in Korean instead: goose, tomato, Swiss, Indian, shooting star. The translators had to rebuild the joke from scratch, and honestly, they nailed it.

**The whales.** Young-woo loves whales. Not "thinks they're neat" loves them — encyclopedia-level loves them. Narwhals, belugas, blue whales, killer whales, all of it. She will stop a serious conversation to share a whale fact. And when a legal answer suddenly clicks in her head, the show shows you a whale swimming through the air. It sounds ridiculous on paper. On screen it is the best visual idea in the drama.

**The gimbap.** She eats gimbap almost every meal. The reason given in the show is that gimbap is reliable — every ingredient is visible at a glance. Her father runs a gimbap restaurant, which helps.

**The small rituals.** She is sensitive to noise and touch, avoids eye contact, speaks in a flat tone, and walks with an unusual gait. She closes her eyes and counts to three before walking through a door, to soften the shock of entering a new space.

By episode two you will be counting to three with her.

**Kwon Min-woo** is the character you will argue about. He is not a cartoon villain. He is a guy who thinks the playing field is unfair *to him*. That is a much more uncomfortable kind of antagonist, and the show knows it.

## Why It Went Global

The Netflix run tells the story as clearly as the Korean ratings do.

The show did not even make Netflix's top ten in its first week. Then it hit No. 1 on the global non-English TV chart for the week of July 4–10 with 23.95 million hours viewed. That same week it landed in the top ten in 12 countries and took the No. 1 spot in three of them, including Korea and Taiwan. The next week, July 11–17, viewing nearly doubled to 45.58 million hours, and it stayed at No. 1.

It slipped to No. 2 for the week of July 18–24, losing out to the Spanish series *Alba*. Here is the odd part: its own viewing that week still went *up*, to 55.07 million hours. It just ran into a bigger week. It was back at No. 1 immediately after and stayed there. By the week of August 15–21 it was pulling 77.4 million hours — enough to make it the single most-viewed title anywhere on Netflix that week, ahead of the English-language leader *The Sandman*.

In total it finished No. 1 on the non-English TV chart seven times.

At home, the buzz numbers were even more lopsided. Good Data Corporation tracks weekly drama buzz in Korea using news, online communities, social media and video data. *Extraordinary Attorney Woo* was No. 1 in the TV drama category in **every one of the eight weeks it aired** — a clean sweep that, according to industry reporting at the time, only ten dramas had managed since 2015.

Critics went with it too. On Rotten Tomatoes the season holds a 100% critics score across 11 reviews, with the consensus praising Park Eun-bin's performance and the writing.

If you liked how [Queen of Tears](/posts/queen-of-tears-part1/) balanced a big glossy setting with small human moments, this is playing a similar game — just with a law firm instead of a chaebol family.

## What Kind of Show Is It, Really?

Structurally, it is a case-of-the-week legal drama. Young-woo gets a client, hits a wall, has a whale moment, and finds the angle nobody else saw.

But the cases are the point. They are not just puzzles. Each one carries a real social question. Over sixteen episodes the drama takes on prejudice and discrimination against a wide range of people, and it does it seriously without turning grim.

Some episodes are laugh-out-loud funny. One is about a wedding dress disaster. Another involves a small village and a very old tree. And a couple of them will absolutely make you cry on public transport.

The tonal control here is the real skill. This drama can be silly for ten minutes and then devastating for two, and it never feels like whiplash. If you appreciated that quality in [My Mister](/posts/my-mister-deep-dive/) or [Goblin](/posts/goblin-deep-dive/), you already know why it is so hard to pull off.

## The Conversation It Started

We should be honest about this part, because it matters.

The show sparked a real debate in Korea and abroad about how autism is portrayed on screen.

Kim Yong-jik, an attorney and president of the Autism Society of Korea, noted that it is realistically unlikely for an autistic person to have a very high IQ and become a lawyer — but said the approach still helps make people more interested in understanding autism. Officials at the same organization credited the drama with genuinely sparking Korean interest in the subject.

Other criticisms went further. Some viewers pointed out that not every autistic person has the traits Young-woo has. Others objected to the role being played by a non-autistic actor.

One detail worth knowing: for the English dub, Netflix cast **Sue Ann Pien** as Young-woo's voice. Pien is a Taiwanese-American actress who is autistic herself, best known for the Amazon series *As We See It*. The dub arrived on Netflix in October 2022.

So: not a perfect document, and it never claimed to be. But it moved the conversation forward, and it moved it forward for millions of people at once. Both things are true.

## Awards

At the 59th Baeksang Arts Awards in April 2023, *Extraordinary Attorney Woo* received 10 nominations across the television categories, including Best Drama, Best Director, Best Actress and Best Screenplay.

Park Eun-bin took the Daesang — the Grand Prize, the single biggest honor of the night. Yoo In-shik won Best Director.

One correction worth making, since it shows up in a lot of English write-ups: Park Eun-bin did not win Best Actress that night. She was nominated in that category, but the award went to Song Hye-kyo for *The Glory*. Park received the Grand Prize instead.

## Is There a Season 2?

Short answer: it exists on paper, and that is about it.

AStory signed writer Moon Ji-won to a Season 2 script contract in 2023, originally aiming to produce it in 2024. That did not happen. In January 2026, Korean outlet Bloter reported that Season 2 had entered pre-production, with Moon still writing and an industry source saying the project was moving along normally. The same report noted that script delays had pushed the casting timeline back, and that Park Eun-bin had not been approached about casting.

Then in May 2026, Park Eun-bin was asked about it directly. She was promoting a new Netflix series — reuniting, as it happens, with *Attorney Woo* director Yoo In-shik. She said Young-woo is a character she loves and wants to protect, and that she would need a clear reason before signing on. If she could not convince herself, she said, it would be difficult.

That is not a no. It is not a yes either.

As of July 2026, there is no confirmed broadcaster, cast, schedule or release date. Treat Season 2 as in development, not greenlit. AStory has also said it is pursuing remakes in the United States, Japan and Europe.

The good news: Season 1 resolves its central storyline and works as a largely self-contained story, though a few character arcs are left open. There is no cliffhanger holding you hostage.

## 🇰🇷 Why a Show About Autism Hit This Hard in Korea Specifically

The show's cultural impact inside Korea is tied to something the international rollout doesn't fully carry over: Korea's workplace culture has historically offered limited accommodation or visibility for neurodivergent employees, and public conversation about hiring practices for disabled workers was, at the time this aired, still relatively underdeveloped compared to some other countries. A prime-time hit built around an autistic protagonist succeeding — messily, imperfectly, but genuinely — at one of Korea's most prestige-obsessed professions (top-tier corporate law) put a spotlight directly on a conversation Korean disability advocates had been trying to elevate for years.

That's also why the criticism from groups like the Autism Society of Korea (mentioned above) carried real weight domestically — it wasn't a niche complaint, it was part of a national conversation the show itself had helped restart. Woo Young-woo becoming a genuine pop-culture phenomenon, whale motif and all, meant that conversation reached dinner tables that a policy debate alone never would have.
