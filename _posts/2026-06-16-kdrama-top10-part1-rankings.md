## Why "Most Watched" Is Not the Same as "Best"

Before you scroll to the table, one honest caveat. Netflix's own weekly "Top 10" charts, the ones you see on the app's homepage, are based on a point system weighted toward first-week views. This list is different: it ranks by **total cumulative viewing hours**, which rewards long-running hits and shows people rewatch, not just shows that spiked hard in their debut week.

That distinction matters. A slow-burn family drama with 16 hour-long episodes racks up more total hours per viewer than a tense eight-episode thriller, even if the thriller had the bigger opening weekend. Keep that in mind as you read the numbers below — "most hours watched" and "most culturally important" are not always the same list, even if they overlap more often than not.

## 🏆 How Big Is "Big"?

Numbers this large are hard to picture. Let's put them in context.

| Squid Game S1 Hours | What That Means |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| 1,650,450,000 hrs | Over **182,000 years** of total watch time |
| 1,650,450,000 hrs | **2.6×** more than Netflix's next biggest show at the time |
| 1,650,450,000 hrs | #1 in **94 countries** at the same time |

And Queen of Tears? In the first half of 2024, it was Netflix's **most-watched Asian series worldwide** — landing at #14 on Netflix's global Top 25 for the period, ahead of hundreds of English-language originals.

## 🌍 Why Does the World Love K-Dramas?

- **Short and complete** — Most are 16 episodes. No filler. No years of waiting.
- **High production value** — Cinematography, OSTs, and costumes that rival Hollywood.
- **Real emotions** — K-dramas are not afraid to make you cry.
- **Strong characters** — Women and underdogs drive these stories.
- **Netflix's global reach** — Subtitles in 30+ languages means anyone can watch.

## What Global Audiences Latch Onto vs. What Koreans Talk About

Here is something that does not always come through in a straight ranking table: the reason a drama tops this list globally is often not the same reason it mattered inside Korea.

Take **Extraordinary Attorney Woo**. Internationally, most conversation around it centered on the whales — Woo Young-woo's love of them became a genuine meme. Inside Korea, the bigger conversation was about workplace accommodation for autistic employees, and whether Korean companies were actually prepared to hire someone like her in real life. The show landed differently depending on which side of that conversation you were standing on.

Or take **The Glory**. Global audiences mostly talked about the slow-burn revenge plot and Song Hye-kyo's performance. In Korea, the show reopened a much rawer, more specific conversation about school bullying, because Kim Eun-sook wrote it after researching real cases, and several real-life incidents resurfaced in the news cycle around the same time the show aired. It was not just a revenge fantasy here. It was closer to a mirror.

That gap between "why the world watched it" and "why Korea couldn't stop talking about it" is a big part of what we try to dig into across the deep dives linked below. The hours-watched number gets you in the door. It rarely tells you why a show actually stuck.
