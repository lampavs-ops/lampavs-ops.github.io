South Korea has about 51 million people.

Now imagine over **10 million of them** buying a ticket for the same movie.

That's 1 in 5 Koreans. In the film world, that's called a **"10-million movie"** — and it's a very big deal.

This is a guide to the 10 most-watched Korean films of all time. But there's a twist.

> 📌 **A note about this list**
>
> If you look at Korea's full all-time box office, you'll notice something: historical dramas dominate the top spots. Films about ancient battles, Korean War stories, and political coups from the 1970s.
>
> Those are great films — but they're hard to enjoy without knowing the history.
>
> So this list skips the history class. We picked only films where **the genre does the work** — action, comedy, fantasy, zombie, occult, thriller. No background knowledge needed.

## 🎬 The Full Story Behind Every Film

Making a movie that sells 10 million tickets in South Korea is incredibly hard. Getting 10 million people to the same theater — not streaming, not pirating, actually buying a ticket and sitting in a seat — requires word of mouth, repeat viewings, family outings, and social media buzz all happening at once. Here's what each of these ten films is about, why it worked, and the scene you absolutely cannot miss.

## 🥇 #1 — Extreme Job (극한직업, 2019) | 16.26 Million Admissions

![Extreme Job atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-extreme-job.jpg)
*An absurd undercover operation at a fried chicken restaurant made 16 million Koreans laugh out loud*

> *"Five detectives went undercover at a fried chicken restaurant. The chicken got too popular."*

### The One-Line Plot

Five narcotics detectives take over a fried chicken restaurant as cover for a stakeout — and the chicken gets so popular, the restaurant becomes the real problem.

### Why Did 16 Million People See This?

**① The premise is the joke**
"Detectives run a fried chicken restaurant" is already funny before you watch a single frame. The trailer alone made people laugh. In internet terms, this was a film that was destined to become a meme — and it absolutely did.

**② Five actors with perfect chemistry**
Ryu Seung-ryong, Lee Ha-nee, Jin Seon-kyu, Lee Dong-hwi, and Gong Myung each bring a completely different personality to the team. Remove any one of them and the movie falls apart. Together, they're electric.

**③ Lunar New Year timing**
It opened on January 23, 2019 — right at the Lunar New Year holiday. That's when Korean families head to the cinema together. A film that every generation can enjoy, released at the exact moment families want to go out together. The timing was everything.

**④ Korean humor with a universal heart**
It doesn't stay a pure comedy. In the final act, the team comes together in a way that's genuinely moving. You start laughing and end up feeling something real. That combination is hard to pull off — *Extreme Job* does it effortlessly.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The recipe improvement scene.** The detectives start reading customer reviews and tweaking their fried chicken recipe to get better ratings. It's absurd, it's deadpan, it's completely committed — and it perfectly captures why this film works. Ridiculous situation, played with full sincerity.

## 🥈 #2 — Along with the Gods: The Two Worlds (신과함께-죄와 벌, 2017) | 14.41 Million Admissions

![Along with the Gods atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-along-with-gods.jpg)
*Seven trials across seven hells — the most ambitious Korean fantasy film ever made*

> *"What if after you die, you had to stand trial — in seven different courts of hell?"*

### The One-Line Plot

A firefighter dies in the line of duty and must survive seven hellfire trials in the underworld — with three guardian angels defending him — to earn the right to be reincarnated.

### Why Did 14 Million People See This?

**① It hit a nerve that's deeply Korean**
"Where do we go after we die?" is a question everyone has. But this film answers it using Korean mythology, the concept of filial piety (孝, *hyo*), and a very specific kind of guilt that Korean culture understands intimately. For Korean audiences, watching this felt personal. For international audiences, it felt fresh.

**② The CGI was unlike anything Korea had seen**
Fire Hell, Betrayal Hell, Violence Hell — each trial had its own visual world. For a 2017 Korean production, the scale was jaw-dropping. People went to see it partly just to see what Korean cinema could do technically.

**③ Ha Jung-woo, Cha Tae-hyun, Ju Ji-hoon — a perfect trio**
The three guardian angels have completely different personalities: one is strict and serious, one is warm and funny, one is quiet and complicated. The film gives each of them a real arc. By the end, you care about all three as much as you care about the man they're defending.

**④ The sequel strategy worked perfectly**
Part 1 tells the story of the recently deceased. Part 2 (*The Last 49 Days*) tells the backstory of the guardians. Part 1's success made audiences desperate for Part 2, which also crossed 12 million. Combined: **26.68 million admissions** across two films. One of the biggest franchises in Korean cinema history.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The Murder Hell trial.** The guardian has to defend the firefighter against a charge of murder — and the argument hinges on what the difference is between killing someone and sacrificing yourself for someone else. It sounds like a philosophy class. It plays like the most emotional courtroom scene you've ever watched.

## 🥉 #3 — Veteran (베테랑, 2015) | 13.41 Million Admissions

![Veteran atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-veteran.jpg)
*A scruffy detective versus an untouchable billionaire — Korea's collective rage, bottled and released*

> *"A cocky billionaire. A cop who doesn't care how rich you are."*

### The One-Line Plot

When a ruthless chaebol heir brutalizes a truck driver who stood up to him, a rough-around-the-edges veteran detective goes after him — money, lawyers, and political connections be damned.

### Why Did 13 Million People See This?

**① The villain felt real**
Jo Tae-oh (Yoo Ah-in) isn't a cartoon bad guy. He's the kind of arrogant, untouchable wealthy person that Koreans hear about in the news. The audience walked in already primed to hate him. So when the detective finally corners him, the catharsis is massive.

**② Hwang Jung-min is a force of nature**
Detective Seo Do-cheol is not cool. He's not slick. He wears bad clothes, makes dumb jokes, and gets knocked around constantly. But he is completely, stubbornly righteous — and Hwang Jung-min makes you love him for it. It's one of the great Korean action performances.

**③ It's funnier than you expect**
*Veteran* is not a grim film. The detective squad is full of banter and chaos. There are running gags, ridiculous situations, moments of slapstick. The comedy makes the serious moments hit harder, and the serious moments make the comedy feel earned.

**④ Peak summer release**
August 5, 2015. Right in the heart of Korean summer blockbuster season. *Veteran* positioned itself perfectly as the crowd-pleasing action film of the year — and delivered exactly what it promised.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The warehouse fight.** Late in the film, the detective's team and Jo Tae-oh's crew face off in a cramped warehouse. No wire work, no fancy choreography — just raw, ugly, exhausting brawling. It's visceral and satisfying in a way that polished action sequences rarely are. Your palms will sweat.

## #4 — The Host (괴물, 2006) | 13.02 Million Admissions

![The Host atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-the-host.jpg)
*A monster rises from the Han River — and Bong Joon-ho uses it to say something much bigger*

> *"A monster rises from the Han River. It takes a little girl. Her messy family has to get her back."*

### The One-Line Plot

A mutant creature emerges from Seoul's Han River and snatches a young girl. Her clumsy, broke, dysfunctional family has to rescue her — while the government does everything wrong.

### Why Did 13 Million People See This?

**① Bong Joon-ho breaks the rules**
In a normal monster movie, a hero shows up and saves the day. In *The Host*, the Park family is the opposite of a hero. Gang-du (Song Kang-ho) is lazy and slow. His father is old and out of shape. His sister is an athlete who keeps missing her shots. They're a mess — and that makes them completely believable.

**② There's more than one monster**
The creature in the river is terrifying. But the film is equally focused on the US military that caused the mutation and looked away, the Korean government that locked down civilians instead of helping them, and the bureaucratic machine that cares more about protocol than people. In 2006, that felt very timely in Korea.

**③ It owned the summer of 2006**
Opening on July 27, *The Host* became the highest-grossing Korean film ever at the time. It held that record for years. For a generation of Korean moviegoers, this is the film that proved Korean cinema could go anywhere.

**④ After *Parasite*, everyone went back**
When Bong Joon-ho won four Oscars for *Parasite* in 2020, audiences around the world started watching his back catalog. *The Host* got a massive second wave of international viewers — and it held up completely.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The funeral scene.** The family learns that Hyun-seo may be dead. What follows is one of the most unusual grief scenes in cinema — it keeps tilting between devastating sorrow and dark absurdity until you're not sure whether to cry or laugh. That's Bong Joon-ho in a nutshell.

## #5 — The Thieves (도둑들, 2012) | 12.98 Million Admissions

![The Thieves atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-the-thieves.jpg)
*Korea meets Hong Kong in a heist where everyone is lying to everyone — and it's glorious*

> *"Korea, Hong Kong, Macau. The best thieves in Asia — and they all have their own agenda."*

### The One-Line Plot

A crew of Korean and Hong Kong thieves plan to steal a legendary diamond from a Macau casino. Every single person on the team has their own secret agenda.

### Why Did 13 Million People See This?

**① The cast is the marketing**
Jun Ji-hyun. Kim Yun-seok. Lee Jung-jae. Kim Hye-soo. Kim Soo-hyun. Simon Yam from Hong Kong. Each of these actors has their own massive fanbase — and those fanbases barely overlap. Director Choi Dong-hoon assembled a lineup that could sell tickets to completely different audiences at once.

**② Every twist unlocks another twist**
*The Thieves* is a film about betrayal. The moment you think you understand who's working with whom, the floor drops again. It's the kind of movie you want to rewatch immediately after finishing — not because you missed something, but because you want to see how it all fits together.

**③ Jun Ji-hyun's full reinvention**
Before *The Thieves*, Jun Ji-hyun was best known for romantic comedies like *My Sassy Girl*. Here she plays Yanycall — a tightrope-walking thief who is unpredictable, electric, and impossible to take your eyes off. The role completely changed how audiences and directors saw her. *My Love from the Star* (2013) followed, and that became one of the biggest K-dramas in history. *The Thieves* was the turning point.

**④ Korea + Hong Kong = something new**
Korean and Hong Kong cinema had both dominated Asian markets separately for decades. Seeing them combined in a single, stylish production felt genuinely exciting in 2012 — and it still does.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The Macau hotel climax.** Multiple teams converge on the same target from different angles, each with a different plan, each trying to outmaneuver the others. The editing is brilliant — tight, controlled, and relentlessly tense. You genuinely don't know who's going to end up with the diamond until the very last second.

## #6 — Miracle in Cell No.7 (7번방의 선물, 2013) | 12.81 Million Admissions

![Miracle in Cell No.7 atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-cell-no7.jpg)
*A father with an intellectual disability. A daughter who won't give up. A cell block full of criminals with hearts of gold.*

> *"Dad, come get me."*

### The One-Line Plot

Yong-gu, a man with an intellectual disability, is wrongly convicted of murder and thrown in prison. The hardened criminals in Cell No.7 take him in — and help smuggle in his young daughter, Ye-seung.

### Why Did 12 Million People See This?

**① Ryu Seung-ryong's performance is a career milestone**
Before this film, Ryu Seung-ryong was known mostly as a supporting comic actor. *Miracle in Cell No.7* changed everything. His portrayal of Yong-gu is tender, specific, and completely unsentimental — he never plays the disability for easy sympathy. It's one of the finest performances in Korean cinema, and it made audiences fall completely apart.

**② "An innocent person punished" is universal**
The story of a good, powerless person being crushed by a system that doesn't care — that resonates everywhere. You don't need to know anything about Korea to feel the rage and grief building through this film. And when it finally releases, it releases hard.

**③ It earns its tears by being funny first**
The criminals of Cell No.7 helping Yong-gu is played largely for comedy. It's absurd, warm, and surprisingly charming. That humor isn't filler — it's structural. The laughter in the first half makes the heartbreak in the second half land harder than it ever could on its own.

**④ Three countries remade it**
Turkey, Indonesia, and the Philippines all produced their own versions. The Turkish remake became the highest-grossing film in Turkish cinema history at the time. When a story works that well across that many cultures, it's not sentiment — it's a universal truth.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**Ye-seung gets smuggled in.** The Cell No.7 crew hides Yong-gu's daughter in a backpack and sneaks her past the guards. It's ridiculous, tense, and impossibly sweet — and it captures exactly what makes this film tick. You'll be holding your breath and smiling at the same time.

## #7 — The Roundup (범죄도시2, 2022) | 12.69 Million Admissions

![The Roundup atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-roundup.jpg)
*Ma Dong-seok goes to Vietnam. The bad guys don't stand a chance.*

> *"Ma Dong-seok's fists don't need subtitles."*

### The One-Line Plot

Detective Ma Seok-do tracks a Korean serial killer operating out of Vietnam. Jurisdiction is not a concern. Neither are the laws of physics.

### Why Did 12 Million People See This?

**① It was the first film people wanted to see in a theater after COVID**
*The Roundup* opened May 18, 2022. Audiences had been locked out of cinemas for two years. They came back for this — and they came back in massive numbers. It became the first Korean film to cross 10 million admissions in the post-pandemic era. That timing wasn't luck. The film delivered exactly what people needed: pure, uncomplicated, crowd-pleasing entertainment.

**② Ma Dong-seok is a genre unto himself**
After *Train to Busan* and *Crime City* (the first film), Ma Dong-seok's action style had become its own thing. Audiences don't just watch *The Roundup* for a good story. They watch it to see Ma Dong-seok punch someone very large, very hard, in very satisfying ways. The film knows this and commits to it completely.

**③ The sequel is better than the original**
*The Roundup* has a bigger location (Vietnam), a scarier villain, and tighter action set pieces than *Crime City* (2017). Sequels that outperform their predecessors are rare. This is one of them.

**④ It launched an unstoppable franchise**
*Crime City* (2017): 6.88M. *The Roundup* (2022): 12.69M. *The Roundup: No Way Out* (2023): 10.69M. *The Roundup: Punishment* (2024): 11.44M. All four films crossed major milestones. The series is now the most commercially dominant franchise in Korean cinema history.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The final confrontation.** Ma Seok-do vs. the main villain. No wire work. No fancy moves. Just Ma Dong-seok doing what Ma Dong-seok does — pushing forward like a freight train until the problem stops. The whole theater cheers. Every time.

## #8 — Exhuma (파묘, 2024) | 11.91 Million Admissions

![Exhuma atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-exhuma.jpg)
*A grave that should never have been opened. A film that stays with you for days.*

> *"Don't open that grave."*

### The One-Line Plot

A wealthy Korean-American family hires a shaman and a geomancer to relocate an ancestral grave in Korea — hoping to break a generational curse. They should not have moved that grave.

### Why Did 11 Million People See This?

**① It went viral because the experience was too strong to keep quiet**
*Exhuma* had a quiet opening weekend. Then word started spreading. "You have to see this." "I couldn't sleep after." "Watch it in a group." Within two weeks, the theater lines were long. Horror films that spread through word-of-mouth are rare — it means the film delivers something genuinely hard to shake.

**② Korean shamanism as a horror system**
Western horror runs on ghosts, demons, and jump scares. *Exhuma* runs on *musok* — Korean shamanism — and the ancient belief that disturbing the dead has consequences. For international audiences, this is a completely unfamiliar set of rules, which makes it genuinely unpredictable. For Korean audiences, it taps into something cultural and ancestral that feels close to home.

**③ Director Jang Jae-hyun controls dread like a master**
Jang already proved himself with *The Priests* (2015) and *Svaha: The Sixth Finger* (2019). *Exhuma* is his biggest canvas yet. The first half is slow, oppressive, and methodical — building unease without releasing it. The second half pulls the trigger. The structure rewards patience with a payoff that hits genuinely hard.

**④ Four actors at the top of their game**
Choi Min-sik (of *Oldboy* fame) anchors the film as the geomancer. Kim Go-eun throws herself into the shaman role with total physical commitment. Yoo Hae-jin brings warmth and groundedness. Lee Do-hyun adds quiet tension. Each character has a clear function, and each actor delivers.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The first ritual.** Kim Go-eun's shaman performs a full *gut* (shamanic ceremony). The physical performance, the sound design, the lighting — it fills the screen with a kind of energy that makes your skin uncomfortable. You're not sure if you're watching something sacred or something dangerous. That ambiguity is the whole film.

## #9 — Train to Busan (부산행, 2016) | 11.57 Million Admissions

![Train to Busan atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-train-to-busan.jpg)
*Zombies on a bullet train. No exits. The film that made the world pay attention to Korean cinema.*

> *"Zombies on a bullet train. Nowhere to run."*

### The One-Line Plot

A father and his young daughter board the KTX from Seoul to Busan. The zombie apocalypse starts on the train. There is nowhere to go.

### Why Did 11 Million People See This?

**① The setup is a masterpiece of constraint**
Zombies in a contained space with no escape route — the premise does half the work. Every car door becomes a life-or-death decision. Every section of train becomes a different battleground. The geography of the train is used brilliantly from start to finish, and the tension never lets up across the full 118 minutes.

**② The emotional story is stronger than the horror**
Gong Yoo's character, Seok-woo, starts the film as a selfish, workaholic father. His daughter knows it. So do we. The film is really about whether he can become someone his daughter doesn't have to be ashamed of — and it earns every moment of his arc. *Train to Busan* makes you cry. That's not an accident. It's the point.

**③ Ma Dong-seok steals every scene he's in**
Gong Yoo is the lead, but Ma Dong-seok as Sang-hwa — a tough, loudmouthed man who is also the most genuinely decent person on the train — is what audiences remember. His tunnel sequence, punching through a wall of zombies with his bare hands, is legendary. It's where the Ma Dong-seok myth really begins.

**④ It put Korean genre cinema on the global map**
Before *Parasite*, *Train to Busan* was the film that made international audiences take Korean cinema seriously. It played at Cannes midnight screenings to a standing ovation. It was distributed in 167 countries. Hollywood talked about a remake. No Korean genre film had ever traveled that far, that fast.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The tunnel sequence.** The survivors have to move through a pitch-dark tunnel car packed with zombies. Ma Dong-seok leads. The camera barely lets you breathe. It's the most intense 90 seconds in Korean action cinema — and it launched a career.

## #10 — Parasite (기생충, 2019) | 10.31 Million Admissions

![Parasite atmosphere image](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-parasite.jpg)
*Best Picture. Best Director. Best Original Screenplay. Best International Feature. The night Korean cinema changed forever.*

> *"4 Academy Awards. Best Picture. That's the pitch."*

### The One-Line Plot

The Kim family lives in a cramped semi-basement apartment. The Park family lives in a stunning architect-designed house. Through clever schemes, the Kims infiltrate the Parks' lives one by one. Then a basement door opens — and the film becomes something else entirely.

### Why Did 10 Million People See This?

**① The Oscars sent people back to theaters**
*Parasite* won four Academy Awards on February 9, 2020 — including Best Picture, the first time a non-English-language film had ever won. In Korea, it felt like a national moment. Audiences who had already seen it went back. Audiences who hadn't raced to catch up. The Oscar announcement was, functionally, the biggest marketing event in Korean film history.

**② The genre keeps shifting underneath you**
*Parasite* starts as a dark comedy. Then it becomes a thriller. Then it becomes a tragedy. Bong Joon-ho never signals the transitions — you realize what kind of film you're watching only after you're already deep inside it. That instability keeps you tense from the first frame to the last.

**③ The class commentary works everywhere**
"The wealthy can afford to be naive. The poor have to be clever." That's the engine of *Parasite* — and it's not a Korean idea. It's a universal one. Audiences in the US, UK, France, Japan, Brazil all recognized it as their own world. That's why the film crossed borders the way it did.

**④ The symbols reward rewatching**
Stairs going up, stairs going down. The smell that can't be washed away. The rain that floods a basement and fills a mansion's garden with water at the same time. Every visual choice means something. First-time viewers feel it. Repeat viewers understand it. That's a film designed to be seen more than once.

### The Scene You Can't Miss

**The basement door opens.** It divides the film in two. Before it: dark comedy and clever scheming. After it: something much more unsettling. Don't look it up. Don't let anyone tell you what's behind the door. Just let it happen — that moment of surprise is half of what makes *Parasite* work.

### By the Numbers

| | |

## 👀 What This List Says About Korean Cinema

![Korean movies top 10 genre breakdown](/assets/img/posts/kmovie-top10-genre.jpg)
*Crime-action takes three spots. Thrillers of various kinds take four. Korean audiences love satisfaction and surprise — preferably both at once.*

**Nobody goes it alone.** All ten films are built around teams and families, not lone heroes. A detective squad, three guardian angels, a detective and his crew, a dysfunctional family, a crew of thieves, a cell block of criminals. The chemistry between characters is never an afterthought — it's the engine of the whole film.

**Genre-mixing is the default.** *Extreme Job* is action-comedy. *Along with the Gods* is fantasy-family drama. *Veteran* is crime-action with social commentary. *The Host* is monster-family-political satire. *Exhuma* is occult horror rooted in real shamanic belief. None of them stay inside one lane. Korean blockbusters don't seem to believe in pure genres — and emotion consistently beats genre as the real destination: *Train to Busan* is a zombie film that makes you cry about a father and daughter, *Miracle in Cell No.7* is a prison drama that makes you laugh before it breaks you.

**There's always a social subtext.** Chaebol abuse of power (*Veteran*). Government failure (*The Host*). Guilt and family duty (*Along with the Gods*). Class and opportunity (*The Thieves*, *Parasite*). Even the most crowd-pleasing films on this list have something real underneath. That's not an accident.

**Two directors, four films between them.** Bong Joon-ho has two films in the TOP 10 — *The Host* at #4 and *Parasite* at #10, two completely different genres and tones, both in the all-time top tier. Ma Dong-seok appears in two more, *Train to Busan* (#9) and *The Roundup* (#7); at this point, his presence on a film's poster is essentially a box office guarantee.

**Release timing was weaponized.** *Extreme Job* opened on Lunar New Year. *Veteran*, *The Host*, and *The Roundup* all opened in peak summer or the post-COVID reopening window. Korean studios know exactly when families want to go to the cinema — and they schedule accordingly.

**You can watch all of these tonight.** Every film on this list is available on Netflix or major streaming platforms in most countries. No excuses. Pick one and start. 🍿

## 🇰🇷 Why "10 Million" Is Such a Specific Number in Korea

Every film on this list crossed 10 million admissions, and that's not a round number Korean media picked at random. It comes from a simple bit of math: South Korea has roughly 51 million people, so a "10-million movie" (천만 영화, *cheonman yeonghwa*) means, on average, 1 in every 5 Koreans bought a ticket. Korean entertainment news treats crossing that line as its own headline event, separate from the film's total box office revenue — a movie can make a lot of money without ever becoming a "cheonman yeonghwa," and Koreans track the two numbers differently.

Release timing plays into this directly. Korean theaters see two annual surges — Lunar New Year (설날) and Chuseok (추석), the Korean harvest holiday — when extended families travel to be together and going to the movies as a group is a default holiday activity. *Extreme Job*'s Lunar New Year release wasn't a coincidence; Korean studios build release calendars around these windows specifically because family theater trips are so culturally embedded in how the holidays are spent.

One more piece of local context: KOBIS, the source for the admissions numbers above, is a real-time public database run by the Korean Film Council — anyone in Korea can look up a film's daily ticket sales themselves. That transparency is part of why "10 million" carries so much weight as a marker; it's not a studio's self-reported figure, it's a number the whole country can watch climb in real time.

## 🇰🇷 The Korean Habit That Makes These Numbers Possible: 입소문 and N차 관람

Two pieces of Korean moviegoing culture explain how films like these reach 10–16 million admissions in a country of 51 million.

The first is **입소문** (*ipsomun*, literally "mouth-rumor," Korea's word for word-of-mouth). Korean audiences are famously willing to let a film's opening-weekend numbers be modest if the buzz afterward is strong — *Veteran* and *Extreme Job* both built their biggest weeks *after* release, once ipsomun kicked in, rather than front-loading everything into an opening weekend the way many Hollywood blockbusters are designed to. *Exhuma*'s slow-burn word-of-mouth climb worked the exact same way almost a decade later.

The second is **N차 관람** (*N-cha gwallam*, "N-th time viewing") — the practice of watching a film in theaters multiple times, often specifically to catch details missed the first time. It's common enough that Korean multiplex chains like CGV and Megabox run loyalty programs and limited-edition ticket designs aimed squarely at repeat viewers. A film that rewards a second or third watch, the way *The Thieves*' layered twists or *Parasite*'s dense symbolism do, taps directly into this habit — and every repeat ticket counts toward the same 10-million tally as a first-time viewer's.

## 🇰🇷 Two Films, Two Very Different Korean Reactions

*Exhuma* and *Parasite* both traveled internationally, but they landed very differently back home, and that gap is worth knowing about.

*Exhuma* leans on *musok* (무속, Korean shamanism) — a belief system with real, living practitioners in Korea today, not a folklore relic. For Korean audiences, the film's rituals felt uncomfortably plausible rather than purely fictional, which is part of why word-of-mouth pushed it from a quiet opening to a genuine phenomenon. International viewers, without that cultural referent, tended to describe the same scenes as simply "unfamiliar and effective" horror — a real film, just processed as a different kind of unfamiliar.

*Parasite*'s reception split along a different line. Abroad, the class commentary read as universal — critics in the US, France, and Japan all wrote about recognizing their own societies in it. Inside Korea, alongside that same reading, there was an additional layer of very specific recognition: the semi-basement housing, the SKY-university admissions pressure, and the tutoring economy are all things many Korean viewers had direct, lived experience with, not just a message they agreed with intellectually.

Neither reaction is "more correct" than the other. But it's a good reminder that a film's global box office and critical reception don't always tell you how differently a story is actually being felt, depending on where you're sitting in the theater.
